## Existing guardrails: what the evidence now says

- **10% weekly rule.** The only randomised trial (GRONORUN, 532 novices) found no injury reduction from a 10%-rule graded programme: 20.8% vs 20.3% injured [2]. The 2025 Garmin-RUNSAFE cohort (5,205 runners, ~588,000 sessions) found **no relationship** between week-to-week ratio and injury [1]. Cohort data point to harm only at much larger jumps: >30% per week in novices (HR 1.59, borderline, distance-related injuries) [3]; 20–60% in half-marathon trainees (higher risk at 21 days) [4]. A 2018 systematic review called the evidence "very limited" [5].
- **Rolling 4-week (ACWR-style) cap.** Garmin-RUNSAFE found a *negative* dose-response for the acute:chronic ratio [1], so this cap has no support as an injury predictor.
- **What does predict injury:** a single run longer than the longest run of the previous 30 days. Overuse-injury rate was higher for spikes of 10–30% (HRR 1.64), 30–100% (1.52) and >100% (2.28) [1]. The authors say even 1–10% increases may carry some risk, and that the result needs confirming. The cohort's limits: it was observational, average age 46, and 22% female.

**Recommendation.** Keep the 10% weekly cap as a conservative default. It does no harm, but present it as a convention, not evidence. Treat about 20–30% per week as the evidence-based hard limit. Keep the 4-week cap only as a sanity check. **Add a session-spike rule to every plan:** no run more than 10% longer (in distance or time) than the longest run of the last 30 days, and prefer ≤5–10%. Recovery weeks every 3rd–4th week and 75–85% easy running are coaching convention. I didn't find evidence against them, and I didn't review them in depth.

## 1. Units

**Verdict: supported.** A user-selectable imperial/metric setting is standard. Strava, for example, offers it account-wide [12]. Miles as the US default is a reasonable product choice. No change.

## 2. Race week and the 10% rule

**Verdict: supported with changes.**

Evidence:
- The week-to-week ratio didn't predict injury, but single-session spikes did [1]. Weekly totals are the wrong lens for a race, and the session-spike lens is the right one.
- A half or full marathon race is usually longer than the longest training run (for example, 26.2 mi after a 20-mile long run is a ~31% spike). That is a deliberate spike, so the app can't avoid it, only plan around it [1].
- Pfitzinger treats the race and the recovery after it as part of the plan, not as extra load to be capped [9].

Recommended rule:
- Leave the race out of race week's 10% check (as proposed). The taper usually keeps the rest of the week under the cap anyway.
- **Also leave the race out of the baseline for the week after**, so a race-inflated week doesn't allow a bigger next week.
- Run the session-spike check on the race, but as an information flag only, never a block.
- Make the days after the race easy-only, scaled to race distance (for example, at least 3–5 easy or rest days after a half and 7–14 after a marathon). That range is coaching convention. I didn't find a recovery-timing paper I could open.

## 3. Returning from a 1–2 week break

**Verdict: supported with changes.**

Evidence:
- Endurance athletes lose about 4–7% of VO2max after 12–15 days off, and about 10–20% after 5–8 weeks. Losses level off around 12 weeks [6].
- Short-term detraining (under 4 weeks) quickly reduces VO2max and blood volume in trained athletes [7].
- Daniels' VDOT adjustment: no change for up to 5 days off. 14 days off without cross-training means 97.3% of pre-break VDOT (98.6% with cross-training) [8]. That table adjusts paces only and gives no volume figures.
- Pfitzinger: after fewer than 10 missed days, resume the schedule where you should be [9].
- The AIS position statement: the time needed to return to full load scales with the length of the break, and there is no formula [10].

So a fast return after 1–2 weeks fits the coaching sources. No study tests a specific ramp, and tendon and bone deconditioning are less studied than aerobic fitness.

Recommended rule:
- **5 days off or fewer:** resume the plan.
- **6–14 days:** week 1 at about 60–70% of pre-break weekly volume with no hard sessions. Week 2 at about 80–90% with one quality session. Full volume in week 3. That puts the runner back at full volume after about two weeks, as proposed, without starting the first week at 100%.
- Cap the first long run back by the session-spike rule. The 30-day window still includes pre-break runs, so a "normal" long run can pass the check even when the runner isn't ready for it. Trim the long run by the same fraction as the week.
- Paces: apply the Daniels factor, for example about 97% of VDOT after 2 weeks off.
- **Breaks over 2 weeks:** normal progression (as proposed).

## 4. Marathon long run at low weekly volume

**Verdict: supported with changes.**

Evidence:
- Daniels: keep the long run under 25–30% of weekly mileage, capped at 2.5–3 hours (summarised by Hansons) [11].
- Hansons: 25–30% is a "cardinal rule" and 2–3 hours is the useful window. They criticise beginner plans whose 20-mile long run is about 50% of a 40-mile week [11][13].
- Garmin-RUNSAFE ties injury to how a session compares with recent sessions, not to its share of the week [1]. No study tests a long-run share threshold. The 25–35% figures are coaching consensus, not injury data.

Sources disagree here. Mainstream novice plans do go to about 45–50%, and Daniels and Hansons say no. The evidence that exists favours capping the session spike, not the share.

Recommended rule. Use all three of these, with the first one primary:
1. **Spike cap (primary):** no long run more than 10% longer than the longest run in the last 30 days.
2. **Time cap:** about 150 minutes by default, up to 180 minutes for marathon plans. Most low-volume runners will hit this before 20 miles.
3. **Share cap:** a soft warning above 35% of weekly time and a hard ceiling at 45% for marathon plans. Other distances stay at 30–35%.

If the 45% ceiling is regularly binding, the plan should raise weekly volume or extend the build instead.

## 5. After a missed or painful week

**Verdict: supported with changes.**

Evidence:
- Run Clever randomised trial (447 runners): progressing intensity vs progressing volume made no significant difference to injury risk [14]. That gives no strong reason to prefer cutting intensity over cutting volume. Turning hard sessions into easy runs of the same length is still a reasonable, low-risk first step.
- Pfitzinger: after fewer than 10 missed days, don't make up lost runs. Resume the schedule, and run the next few VO2max sessions slightly slower [9].
- The AIS statement: rebuild load iteratively, guided by how the athlete responds [10].
- For pain: a pain-monitoring RCT in Achilles tendinopathy found that continued, pain-guided running caused no harm compared with rest [15]. Modified running is acceptable for mild tendon pain.

Recommended rule:
- Keep the proposed order: hard sessions become easy runs of the same length first, then trim.
- Rebuild over 1–2 weeks, never above the original plan (as proposed).
- The first long run back must pass the session-spike check. Don't jump to the next scheduled long run.
- **Add a pain stop rule:** pain that worsens during a run, changes the runner's gait, or is still there the next morning stops progression and drops the runner to easy-only or rest. If it lasts beyond about a week, advise seeing a clinician. The app shouldn't try to diagnose.

## 6. Regrowth after a cut week

Owner's decisions, 21 Nov 2026 (#107), after this check. SPEC 3.5 rules 6 and 7. No study defines a "cut week" or tests a specific rebuild ramp, so every option here is a heuristic; the question was which heuristic rests on the most evidence.

**What counts as a cut week.** Options considered:
1. A week run at under 90% of plan. No source; an arbitrary line. Not chosen.
2. Pfitzinger: after fewer than 10 missed days, resume the schedule without making up runs [9]. Coaching advice for missed days, with no volume test for a week that was run but short. Not chosen as the definition (the no-make-up part stays in rule 6).
3. **Chosen:** a week is cut only when going straight back to the original plan the next week would break one of two limits: a run more than 10% longer than the longest run of the last 30 days [1], or a weekly rise of more than 20% over the volume actually run. Only the spike rule is a limit with direct injury evidence (one large cohort [1]). The evidence for the 20% rise is weak: Garmin-RUNSAFE found no relationship between week-to-week change and injury [1]; the novice cohort's more-than-30% result is borderline [3]; the half-marathon cohort found one association in the 20 to 60% band [4]; and the 2018 review called the evidence "very limited" [5]. 20% is the bottom of the band [4] links to harm, and its value is a heuristic. Otherwise the plain rules (SPEC 3.5 rule 2) apply.

**How the plan grows back.** Options considered:
1. Return steps alone (rule 4's 70/90/100% or the pain return's 65/82.5/100%), from Pfitzinger [9] and the AIS (return time scales with the break, no formula) [10]. Coaching convention, not injury data. Not chosen alone; used as a floor in the chosen rule.
2. A symptom-gated graded return to run (Warden et al. 2016, as adapted in the BJSM 2019 Table D [20]): advance a stage only when symptom-free during the run, after it and the next day; if symptoms come back, stop and drop back a stage after a rest day; once running is restored, raise weekly volume by 10 to 20%. Built for bone stress injury, so too slow for a missed week. Not chosen for regrowth (its symptom gate already informs the pain return in rule 7).
3. A rolling acute:chronic workload ratio cap. Rejected: Garmin-RUNSAFE found a negative dose-response for it [1], and a 2021 Frontiers in Physiology editorial (Zouhal et al.) calls the ACWR literature controversial and reports a study in its collection that found no support for a 0.8 to 1.3 "sweet spot".
4. 20% over the previous week actually run, for everything. About 8 weeks back to plan after a very low week, and never after a fully missed one (20% of 0 is 0). Not chosen.
5. Return steps only after a week below 25% of plan (a break week), 20% otherwise. Leaves a cliff at 25%: a week just above it returns far slower than one just below. Not chosen.
6. **Chosen by the owner:** each regrowth week may grow to the higher of 20% over the previous week actually run (a default heuristic, weak evidence as above) and the matching return step, 65%, then 82.5%, then 100% of the original plan, counted from the first week after the cut week; never above plan, every run passing the spike rule (the only limit with direct injury evidence, from one large cohort [1]), rule 7's guards on top, and the lower of this and rule 4's ramp where both apply. Measured against the original plan until a week is back at plan (also after a short-break ramp that ends below plan). A fully missed or very low week is back at plan within about 3 weeks, and no low week returns slower than the pain return's steps. Inside a regrowth, a week run at or above 1/1.2 (about 83%, derived from the 20% default) of its own planned volume continues the count; a week run short of that is a new cut week and the count restarts at step 1 from the week run. The bound replaces the plain 10% check for regrowth weeks only.

**Pain stops over 14 days.** Measuring rule 2's checks and rule 6's 20% bound from the stop weeks would keep the runner near the 20-minute test run indefinitely. Chosen: the return's own ceilings (65%, 82.5% and 100% of the pre-stop level) plus the spike rule bound those weeks; rule 7's other guards still apply. Known consequence: a stop of more than 14 days can reach 100% a week sooner than a 6 to 14 day stop, whose rule 4 ramp caps week 3 at about 11% over week 2.

**Longer non-pain breaks** (more than 14 days off, or three or more break weeks) keep rule 4's "rules 1 and 2 as written" and are not cut weeks; their slow return is a known gap, open as #111.
