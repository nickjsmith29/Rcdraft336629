# Daily readiness: HRV, resting HR and sleep (SPEC 3.4 and F5, #3)

Evidence check for the daily readiness rule, read on 24 Nov 2026. This is a recovery rule, so the owner must approve it before it is built (the research rule in CLAUDE.md). Every threshold in the rule is a heuristic; no study tests a readiness rule built from Apple Watch data.

Source numbers in brackets match SPEC.md section 12. [35] and [36] were already named in SPEC 3.4 without a number; they are numbered here.

## Summary

## Sources read

### HRV-guided training trials and reviews

**[35] Granero-Gallegos A, González-Quílez A, Plews D, Carrasco-Poyatos M. HRV-based training for improving VO2max in endurance athletes. A systematic review with meta-analysis. Int J Environ Res Public Health 2020 (PMC7663087).** Already cited in 3.4. Six RCTs (Javaloyes 2019, Kiviniemi 2007, Kiviniemi 2010, Nuuttila 2017, Schmitt 2018, Vesterinen 2016), 195 participants, 17 comparisons. VO2max effect 0.402 (95% CI 0.273 to 0.531) for HRV-guided training against 0.215 (0.101 to 0.329) for predefined training; heterogeneity high (I² 94%). Risk of bias rated "unclear" in its table and high for all but one study in its text. It describes the decision rules only as directions: Kiviniemi's groups did moderate exercise or rest when HRV decreased. Evidence: weak to moderate.

**[37] Kiviniemi AM, Hautala AJ, Kinnunen H, Tulppo MP. Endurance training guided individually by daily heart rate variability measurements. Eur J Appl Physiol 2007 (doi:10.1007/s00421-007-0552-2).** RCT, 26 moderately fit men, 4 weeks of 40-minute runs. Morning HRV (high-frequency power). Rule: high-intensity training when HRV was unchanged or up; low intensity or rest when it fell below the 10-day mean minus 1 SD, or fell for 2 days in a row. Maximal running velocity rose more in the HRV group (about 0.9 against 0.5 km/h, P = 0.048); VO2peak rose only in the HRV group (56 to 60 ml/kg/min) but the between-group difference was not significant. Small sample, one-sided rule.

**[38] Vesterinen V, Nummela A, Heikura I, et al. Individual endurance training prescription with heart rate variability. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2016.** Read from the author repository's accepted manuscript. 40 recreational runners, 31 completed; 4 weeks of preparation then 8 weeks of intensive training. HRV: 4 minutes supine on waking with a chest strap, RMSSD (no log), 7-day rolling average. The smallest worthwhile change was the mean ± 0.5 × SD of the 7-day rolling average over the 4 preparation weeks, recomputed after 4 weeks. Outside the band, either side: low intensity or rest until the 7-day average came back to the band's mean. The HRV group did fewer moderate and hard sessions (13.2 against 17.7) and improved 3000 m speed by 2.1% (P = 0.004) against 1.1% (not significant); between-group effects were small (ES 0.42 for 3000 m, 0.26 for VO2max).

**[39] Javaloyes A, Sarabia JM, Lamberts RP, Moya-Ramon M. Training prescription guided by heart-rate variability in cycling. Int J Sports Physiol Perform 2019 (doi:10.1123/ijspp.2018-0122).** Read from the accepted manuscript (accepted May 2018). 17 well-trained cyclists, 4 baseline weeks then 8 training weeks. Morning supine 60 s (after 30 s discarded) with a chest strap, ln rMSSD, 7-day rolling average. Band: baseline mean ± 0.5 × SD of ln rMSSD, recomputed after 4 weeks. Inside: moderate or hard sessions; outside: low intensity or rest; never more than two hard days or two rest days in a row. The HRV group improved peak power (+5.1%), VT2 power (+13.9%) and 40-minute time trial (+7.3%); between-group differences were not significant (only "likely beneficial" by magnitude-based inference).

**[40] Düking P, Zinner C, Trabelsi K, Reed JL, Holmberg HC, Kunz P, Sperlich B. Monitoring and adapting endurance training on the basis of heart rate variability monitored by wearable technologies: a systematic review with meta-analysis. J Sci Med Sport 2021;24(11):1180–1192.** Abstract read. 8 studies, 198 participants, 9 interventions. HRV-guided training: medium effect on submaximal measures (g = 0.296, 95% CI 0.031 to 0.562), small and non-significant effects on performance (g = 0.079) and VO2peak (g = 0.171); fewer performance non-responders. HRV-guided groups usually did fewer hard sessions. So the main gain is doing hard sessions on better days, not more of them.

**[41] Düking P, Zinner C, Reed JL, Holmberg HC, Sperlich B. Predefined vs data-guided training prescription based on autonomic nervous system variation: a systematic review. Scand J Med Sci Sports 2020;30(12):2291–2304.** Full text read (university repository). Five studies, six interventions, 4 to 8 weeks, all PEDro 7, none blinded; the authors call the evidence "limited and inconsistent" and their conclusions "somewhat preliminary". Baselines differed (7-day moving averages, 10-day baselines) and "no consensus" exists on the baseline period. Measuring "at least 3 or 4 times each week should be feasible"; morning measurement recommended; "only a few wearable sensors" are validated.

### Rolling averages and the normal range

**[42] Plews DJ, Laursen PB, Kilding AE, Buchheit M. Evaluating training adaptation with heart-rate measures: a methodological comparison. Int J Sports Physiol Perform 2013.** Abstract read. 10 runners, 9 weeks. Single-day ln rMSSD and resting HR correlated trivially to moderately with changes in fitness and 10 km performance (for example r = -0.06 with maximal aerobic speed); weekly averages correlated very largely (r = 0.72 for ln rMSSD, -0.62 for resting HR with maximal aerobic speed; 0.73 for resting HR with 10 km change). This is the direct evidence behind "never act on one HRV reading", and it applies to resting HR too.

**[43] Plews DJ, Laursen PB, Le Meur Y, Hausswirth C, Kilding AE, Buchheit M. Monitoring training with heart-rate variability: how much compliance is needed for valid assessment? Int J Sports Physiol Perform 2014;9(5).** Abstract read. Trained triathletes; averages of 1 to 7 randomly chosen days a week. The standardized change in ln rMSSD plateaued after about 3 days and the correlation with performance after about 4; recommends at least 3 valid measurements a week.

**[44] Buchheit M. Monitoring training status with HR measures: do all roads lead to Rome? Front Physiol 2014;5:73.** Full text read. Day-to-day variation of ln rMSSD is large (CV about 10 to 20%), and resting HR's typical error is about 10% in its Table 1, so single values have a poor signal-to-noise ratio. Smallest worthwhile changes used in the literature: 0.5 × or 1 × the individual CV; "no evidence" that any fraction is practically meaningful. Best practice: 5 to 10 minute recordings on waking; nocturnal recordings are "noisy and difficult to implement daily". A fall in vagal HRV is not always fatigue: it happens in "saturation" (well-trained athletes with very low resting HR) and in tapers, and telling these apart needs HR and HRV together. Plews's correlations appeared only with "the average of at least 3–4 days".

**[53] Altini M. What's your normal range for heart rate variability (HRV)? (Substack, HRV4Training.)** Coaching practice, not peer-reviewed. HRV4Training builds the normal range from the last 60 days of the runner's own measurements; a baseline below normal marks "a day or period of increased stress", a reason to reduce "training intensity or other stressors"; the goal is "normal", not "higher". This is the origin of the 60-day range the owner chose.

### Apple Watch measurement

**[36] O'Grady B, Lambe R, Baldwin M, Acheson T, Doherty C. The validity of Apple Watch Series 9 and Ultra 2 for serial measurements of heart rate variability and resting heart rate. Sensors 2024;24(19):6220.** Already cited in 3.4 (as Doherty et al., the last author). 39 healthy adults, 316 morning measurements: 5 minutes supine on waking, Apple Watch (Breathe app) against a Polar H10 with Kubios. SDNN: mean absolute error 20.5 ms (MAPE 28.9%), equivalence within ±10 ms not met; resting HR: mean absolute error 3.7 bpm (MAPE 5.9%), mean bias about 0. It notes Apple computes SDNN "every two to four hours" in the background, which this study did not test. The authors suggest use for trends, not absolute values.

**[49] Apple Support. Monitor your heart rate with Apple Watch (HT204666).** Read on 24 Nov 2026. "HRV is sampled while you're still, so the number of measurements you see will vary according to your activity level." It does not say how resting HR is computed.

**[48] Apple Support. Track your overnight and daytime vitals with Apple Watch (120142), and the Apple Watch user guide page on Vitals.** Read on 24 Nov 2026. The Vitals app builds a "typical range" for overnight heart rate, respiratory rate, wrist temperature, blood oxygen and sleep duration (HRV on the newest watches) after 7 nights of wear, and notifies by default when at least two overnight metrics are outside the typical range, naming possible factors such as illness, alcohol, medication and elevation. Apple's own design therefore also waits for two signals.

**[54] Altini M. On heart rate variability and the Apple Watch. Medium, 2018.** Coaching practice. Apple Health stores only SDNN; the watch writes it during Breathe sessions and at unclear other times of day or night. Daytime spot readings have "very little repeatability" and mostly reflect acute stressors (activity, coffee, walking); a reading taken in a known context, on waking or at night, is worth more than many random ones.

**What this means for the method.** The trials measured rMSSD from a chest strap every morning under the same conditions. HealthKit gives SDNN from the watch, a few times a day or night while the runner is still, with a ~29% mean error against a strap even when the conditions are standardized [36]. So the spec: uses only overnight samples (inside the night's sleep, or 00:00 to 06:00 when no sleep is recorded), which is the nearest to a standard condition the watch offers; takes the log, as [39][42][43] did for rMSSD (the log has not been tested on SDNN; heuristic); averages over 7 nights with at least 3 [43]; and never lets HRV count as more than one signal or act alone. The range half-width is 0.5 SD of the nightly values, not of the 7-night averages as in [38]: the SD of 7-night averages is smaller, so [38]'s band flags more often, and with Apple's sparse, noisy SDNN a band that narrow would flag much of the time. Because a second signal is always needed, the wider band only matters when another signal agrees; it is one of the owner questions on #3's spec PR.

### Resting heart rate

**[46] Bosquet L, Merkari S, Arvisais D, Aubert AE. Is heart rate a convenient tool to monitor over-reaching? A systematic review of the literature. Br J Sports Med 2008;42(9):709–714.** Abstract read. Short overload (2 weeks or less) moderately raised resting HR; longer overload left it unchanged. The changes are small to moderate, may sit inside normal day-to-day variation, and should be read with other signs.

**[45] Bellenger CR, Fuller JT, Thomson RL, Davison K, Robertson EY, Buckley JD. Monitoring athletic training status through autonomic heart rate regulation: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Sports Med 2016 (doi:10.1007/s40279-016-0484-2).** Abstract read. Resting vagal HRV rose slightly with better performance (RMSSD SMD 0.58) and still rose slightly or didn't change with overreaching (RMSSD SMD 0.26; HF and SD1 trivial), so resting HRV alone does not tell overreaching from adaptation. This supports weighting HRV no more than the other signals.

**[47] Alavi A, Bogu GK, Wang M, et al. Real-time alerting system for COVID-19 and other stress events using wearable data. Nat Med 2021 (doi:10.1038/s41591-021-01593-2).** Read on the journal page. The NightSignal algorithm compares each night's average overnight resting HR (midnight to 07:00) with the person's own baseline, the streaming median of all earlier nights (stable after about 7 nights for over 80% of people). Yellow alert: 3 bpm above baseline; red: at least 4 bpm above on two nights in a row. 3 bpm was chosen because the median fluctuation of the nightly medians over 3 months was 3 bpm (SD about 0.8). Alerts clustered from about 4 days before to 11 days after symptoms, median 3 days before. Observational, smartwatch overnight HR rather than Apple's daily resting HR, and for illness, not training fatigue. It is the best source found for a resting-HR threshold relative to a personal baseline.

What the spec takes: a +3 bpm rise (the yellow, more sensitive line) on the two latest consecutive days, against the median of the 60 days before them. Apple's daily resting HR has about 3.7 bpm error per reading but no bias [36], so requiring two days matters. Apple's resting HR is not strictly overnight; that difference is a weak point.

### Sleep

**[50] Watson NF, Badr MS, Belenky G, et al. Recommended amount of sleep for a healthy adult: a joint consensus statement of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society. Sleep 2015.** Read from the AASM PDF. "Adults should sleep 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis to promote optimal health." Less than 7 hours habitually is linked to worse health and performance; more than 9 may suit young adults, people recovering from sleep debt and people who are ill. 15 experts, modified RAND method, 5,314 papers. It does not address athletes and sets no 6-hour line.

**[51] Craven J, McCartney D, Desbrow B, et al. Effects of acute sleep loss on physical performance: a systematic and meta-analytical review. Sports Med 2022;52:2669–2690.** Read on the journal page. 69 publications. Acute sleep loss lowered performance overall by 7.6% (95% CI 3.1 to 11.9) and endurance by 5.6% (3.0 to 8.1; 20 studies, 237 people); restriction that cuts the end of the night (waking early) hurt, restriction at the start of the night was inconsistent; about 0.4% lost per extra hour awake before exercise. Very high heterogeneity, mostly male samples, sleep quality rarely measured. Moderate evidence that one short night costs endurance performance, so a single night can count as a signal, unlike a single HRV reading.

**[52] Walsh NP, Halson SL, Sargent C, et al. Sleep and the athlete: narrative review and 2021 expert consensus recommendations. Br J Sports Med 2021.** Read from the author repository's manuscript (the last few pages of the supplement were not read). 7 to 9 h for healthy adults, athletes may need more, and an individual approach based on the athlete's own needs is preferred over a single number. The effect of 1 to 3 nights of partial restriction on performance is "unclear" in its reading. Research and consumer wearables "typically overestimate sleep duration" against polysomnography. No rule for changing training after a poor night.

What the spec takes: two lines, both heuristics. Under 6 hours asleep is short whatever the runner's usual. Under 7 hours (the [50] floor) is short only when it is also at least 60 minutes below the runner's own usual (median of up to 28 earlier nights, at least 7), so a runner who habitually sleeps about 6.5 hours is not flagged every day, and a runner who usually sleeps 8.5 is not flagged at 7.5. Wearables overestimate sleep [52], so a night they measure as short was probably short.

### What coaches do on a poor-readiness day

- The trials replaced the planned moderate or hard session with low-intensity training or rest [37][38][39]; none tested a shortened version of the session. [39] also never allowed more than two hard days in a row.
- **[55] Wegerif S. HRV-guided training. TrainingPeaks coach blog, 2021 (updated 2024).** Coaching practice from the maker of an HRV app. Close to or above baseline: train as planned; "significantly below": "lower intensity or complete rest are advised, depending on how long the dip has continued"; a rolling 7 to 10 day average to read trends. Rest is kept for longer dips, not one low reading.
- Altini [53] and HRV4Training: reduce intensity or other stress on below-normal days; no rule for moving a session.
- Pfitzinger [9] (already in the spec): missed runs are not made up, which the spec applies to the time readiness removes.

No source says how much to shorten a session or where to move it, so the amber and red actions are product heuristics built on F4's existing missed-session slot rule.

## Weak points

- No study tests readiness rules on Apple Watch SDNN, on Apple's daily resting HR, or on wearable sleep; all thresholds are borrowed from chest-strap morning rMSSD trials, an illness-detection study and a sleep consensus.
- The HRV trials are small (17 to 40 people), short (4 to 8 weeks) and unblinded, and their between-group effects are mostly small or non-significant [35][40][41].
- The two-sided HRV band follows [38], which acted outside the band on either side, and [39]'s "outside the band" (its manuscript doesn't say which side); [37] and HRV4Training [53] act only on falls, and [44] warns that a fall is not always fatigue. Counting both sides is the conservative reading, and it only acts with a second signal.
- The +3 bpm line comes from overnight HR in an illness study [47], not training fatigue, and Apple's resting HR is computed differently.
- The 6-hour and 60-minute sleep lines are heuristics; [52] finds the effect of a few nights of partial restriction unclear, while [51] finds a clear average cost.
- How much amber shortens a session (a third of the work, 80% of a long run) and red's half-length long run have no source.

That stays the F1 baseline report's and F13 dashboard's display. Readiness needs its own figures: HRV from overnight samples grouped by night, in ln, against the 60 nights before the 7-night window, with a 0.5 SD band; resting HR against the median of the 60 days before the two latest days.
